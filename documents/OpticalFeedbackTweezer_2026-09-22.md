# Feedback-guided optical vortex experiment

Run `python tools/optical_feedback_tweezer.py --config tools/config/optical_feedback_tweezer.json`. The runner prepares one +1 vortex in darkness, clones that same state, then compares three branches: a beam that follows the vortex, a beam fixed at its initial position, and no beam. The feedback beam targets the previous accepted vortex center plus the configured offset (currently 17.5 nm in +y). It moves toward that target no faster than the configured beam speed. This is an **externally controlled laser path**, not a new force term in TDGL, and it uses no future vortex position.

The default 500 nW absorbed power and 10 nm Gaussian spot reproduce the recent stationary-gradient sweep. In `benchmark_results/optical_gradient_probe_15/`, 17.5 nm gave the largest measured shift among 12.5, 15, and 17.5 nm, but **all three branches crossed the configured 15.5 K critical temperature around step 512**. The new feedback configuration therefore stops at 15.45 K. It should be read as a finite subcritical experiment, not proof that this power can sustain long-time dragging. Lower power, optical duty cycle, or stronger heat removal may be needed for longer runs.

Each numbered result directory contains `dynamic_diagnostics.csv`, `stationary_diagnostics.csv`, `dark_diagnostics.csv`, `summary.json`, `motion_and_gradient.png`, `beam_offset_error.png`, `film_snapshots.png`, and `feedback_motion.gif`. The GIF shows electron temperature, order-parameter amplitude, and phase, with a cyan beam marker and red subcell vortex marker. The CSV records the commanded and evaluated laser positions, the complex-order-parameter zero, the independent amplitude-fit center, electron/phonon temperatures, local thermal and GL-coefficient gradients, beam-vortex distance, and temperature-peak lag. Compare dynamic and stationary vortex displacement at the same completed step; a constant separation by itself does not establish transport.

The vortex center is the bilinear complex-`psi` zero within the unique +1 winding plaquette. This resolves motion below the 1 nm grid spacing, but it remains an interpolation of a discretized TDGL field. The background geometry is a 100 nm unpinned NbN film with zero injected current, fixed applied field, phenomenological two-temperature coefficients, and magnetic self-field feedback disabled. The experiment does not yet model calibrated optical absorption, stochastic fluctuations, quasiparticle transport, or the electromagnetic optical carrier. Its laser heats the electron bath; the vortex response emerges from TDGL and the thermal field. A finite speed cap and sampled feedback can change the observed path even when the underlying physics is unchanged.

For a short validation run, use `--dark-steps 5 --max-steps 10`. For a shorter physical probe with the default dark preparation, use `--max-steps 300`. Outputs reserve a numbered directory and do not overwrite earlier runs.

## Interpretation of the first 3 ps run and next experiment

In `benchmark_results/optical_feedback_tweezer_1`, the commanded beam–vortex separation was 17.5 nm and the final measured separation was 17.493588 nm: an error of 0.006412 nm (0.037%). The controller successfully followed the core. The beam moved 0.890478 nm; the dynamic vortex advanced 0.912151 nm relative to dark, versus 0.914546 nm with a stationary beam. The dynamic branch therefore moved **0.002395 nm less** than the stationary control. This run demonstrates geometric tracking, not useful dragging. The beam moved by less than one tenth of the 10 nm spot sigma, so the two illuminated branches had nearly identical forcing during this short exposure.

The measured electron-temperature gradient at the dynamic vortex rose from roughly zero to +0.04156 K/nm along the beam direction. The gridded temperature maximum ended about 0.87 nm *behind* the beam in y. Its position is quantized by the 1 nm grid, so a continuous peak-location estimate would be preferable. The direction and growth of the gradient are consistent with attraction toward the warm region, but do not establish that the observed subcell drift was caused by this gradient; dark-subtracted and stationary controls remain necessary. The earlier 17.5 nm optimum was only the largest displacement among a limited set of offsets, all of which exceeded Tc during the longer sweep. It is not a validated sustainable optimum.

Veshchunov et al., [*Optical manipulation of single flux quanta*](https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms12801) (2016), attribute subcritical optical manipulation to the temperature dependence of vortex free energy: a temperature gradient drives a vortex toward warmer material, and transport occurs when that drive overcomes pinning. They used a continuous-wave beam; an optical carrier resonance is not required by this thermal mechanism. Their niobium film, micron-scale beam, powers, and thermal times are not quantitative targets for this 100 nm NbN simulation. Their protocol also distinguishes subcritical single-vortex dragging from stronger illumination that creates a normal region.

The next diagnostic should map offset, absorbed power, and time against (1) the **local gradient at the vortex**, (2) its dark-subtracted velocity after a clearly defined thermal transient, (3) the signed temperature-peak lag, (4) peak electron temperature and superconducting connectivity, and (5) whether a vortex stays unique and within the film. Use paired stationary and dark branches from the same prepared state. Select a sustainable operating window before optimizing a controller. A useful controller should regulate the *thermal-gradient direction and magnitude at the vortex* and advance the beam no faster than the measured transport response; it should slow or pause if separation increases or the temperature approaches Tc. Maximizing raw instantaneous velocity alone can favor background drift or near-critical heating. A later independent pinning site is needed to test the published depinning mechanism rather than only motion in an unpinned film.
